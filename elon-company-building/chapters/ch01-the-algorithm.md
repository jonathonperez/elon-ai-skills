# Chapter 1: The Algorithm

## Core Idea
Elon's five-step engineering process — applied in strict order — is the single most important idea across both episodes. It was forged during Tesla's 2018 production hell and is now recited "like a mantra" at every company he runs.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Algorithm (five commandments, order is critical)**:
  1. **Question every requirement** — each must carry the *name of a person*, never a department ("legal" or "safety" is not an answer). Requirements from smart people are the most dangerous because nobody questions them. "Always do so even if the requirement came from me." Make requirements less dumb.
  2. **Delete any part or process you can** — if you don't end up adding back at least 10% of what you deleted, you didn't delete enough.
  3. **Simplify and organize** — only after deleting. "A common mistake is to simplify and optimize a part or process that should never exist."
  4. **Accelerate cycle time** — every process can be sped up, but only after steps 1–3. "If you're digging your grave, don't dig it faster."
  5. **Automate** — last. The Nevada/Fremont mistake was automating first.
  - When to use: any product, process, or system design; run it in nightly meetings when something is urgent.

- **Postscripts to the Algorithm** (Elon's accompanying rules):
  1. All technical managers must have hands-on experience (software managers code ≥20% of their time) — "otherwise they are like a cavalry leader who can't ride a horse."
  2. Camaraderie is dangerous — it stops people challenging each other's work.
  3. It's okay to be wrong; just don't be confident *and* wrong.
  4. Never ask your troops to do something you're not willing to do.
  5. Skip-level meetings: solve problems with the level *below* your managers.
  6. Hire for attitude; skills can be taught, "attitude change requires a brain transplant."
  7. A maniacal sense of urgency is our operating principle.
  8. The only rules are the laws of physics; everything else is a recommendation.

## Worked Example
The fiberglass strips on Model 3 battery packs: an expensive, slow robot glued strips onto packs, bottlenecking the line. Elon asked what the strips were for. Engineering said the noise-reduction team specified them; the noise team said engineering specified them for fire risk — "like being in a Dilbert cartoon." He ordered a sound recording with and without the strips: no difference. Delete. Same with the plastic caps protecting battery pins in transit — nobody could name who required them; deleted, and pins never bent. Both times the requirement had no owner, which is why step 1 demands a name.

The reverse lesson: Tesla's over-automated line "didn't work" — they sawed robots out and threw them into the parking lot, cutting a hole in the building to remove equipment. Automation came first, so it automated waste.

## Anti-patterns
- **Optimizing something that shouldn't exist**: Elon's stated biggest personal mistake — he "spent a lot of time accelerating processes that should have been deleted."
- **Accepting departmental requirements**: no name attached = no accountability = requirement never questioned.
- **Running steps out of order**: "Many things at Tesla were automated, accelerated, simplified, and then deleted."

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the five steps in order; the order is the whole point.
2. Every requirement needs a named owner who takes responsibility for it.
3. If you never re-add deleted things, you're deleting too conservatively.
4. Repeat the algorithm "to an annoying degree" — Elon calls himself a broken record on it deliberately.
